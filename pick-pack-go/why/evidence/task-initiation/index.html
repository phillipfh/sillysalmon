    Task initiation and executive function

    The research behind The hardest part of packing is starting.

    What the essay claims

    "That cluster of mental skills is called executive function, and it's precisely the cluster ADHD makes expensive. A blank packing list asks you to do the hardest part — generating structure from nothing — before you've packed a single sock."

    The construct

    Executive function is the label for the neurocognitive processes that, in the words of the largest meta-analysis of the theory, "maintain an appropriate problem-solving set to attain a later goal" — inhibition, working memory, planning, sustained attention, set shifting.

    "Task initiation" is not one of the domains that meta-analysis measures. It is a category from self-report instruments: the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function–Adult Version (BRIEF-A) has an Initiate scale, on which a person rates how much difficulty they have starting things. That distinction runs through this whole page, and it is the honest version of the essay's claim: "the hardest part is starting" is strongly supported by how adults with ADHD describe themselves, and much less directly by how they perform on laboratory tasks. Those are different kinds of evidence and this page does not merge them.

    What the research says

    • Across 83 studies (ADHD n = 3,734; controls n = 2,969), groups with ADHD showed significant impairment on all executive-function tasks, with effect sizes in the medium range (.46–.69) and the strongest and most consistent effects on response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning. (Willcutt et al., 2005)
    • In a meta-analysis restricted to 13 studies of adults, medium effect sizes appeared in executive domains — verbal fluency d = 0.62, inhibition d = 0.64 and 0.89, set shifting d = 0.65. (Boonstra et al., 2005)
    • Neither meta-analysis includes a measure of task initiation.
    • Among 36 treatment-naive adults with newly diagnosed ADHD, four of the five scales making up the BRIEF-A Metacognition Index — Initiate, Working Memory, Plan/Organize and Task Monitor — were clinically elevated on self-report (defined as T ≥ 65); Organization of Materials was not. (Grane et al., 2014)
    • In that same study the association between laboratory task performance and everyday executive ratings was modest, which the authors read as the two measures representing "non-redundant features of adult ADHD." (Grane et al., 2014)
    • Among 1,090 adults consecutively referred for ADHD evaluation, 44% met a BRIEF-A cutoff for executive dysfunction. (Biederman et al., 2022)

    What it doesn't say

    Executive-function deficits are not universal in ADHD, and the largest meta-analysis says so in its own conclusion. Willcutt et al. write that "moderate effect sizes and lack of universality of EF deficits among individuals with ADHD suggest that EF weaknesses are neither necessary nor sufficient to cause all cases of ADHD," and that difficulties with EF "appear to be one important component of the complex neuropsychology of ADHD." Biederman et al. put a number on the same point from the other direction: 56% of referred adults did not meet the executive-dysfunction cutoff.

    It doesn't say the deficits are specific to executive function. Boonstra et al. found effects of similar size in non-executive domains (consistency of response d = 0.57, word reading d = 0.60, colour naming d = 0.62) and concluded that neuropsychological difficulties in adult ADHD "may not be confined to executive functioning," calling for better-designed executive-function tests.

    Willcutt et al. is not an adult study. Its 83 studies span childhood and adulthood; the adult-specific picture comes from Boonstra et al. (13 studies) and is thinner.

    Self-report and informant report disagree. In Grane et al., the mean scores from the people who knew the participants — partners, family — did not reach the clinical cutoff on any scale, while self-report reached it on seven. The Initiate scale is a person's account of their own difficulty starting; it is not an independent measurement of it. The sample was 36 adults with ADHD and 35 controls in Norway, scored against US norms, which the authors flag as a limitation.

    Nothing here measured packing, and no study has tested whether editing a pre-filled list is easier than writing one from scratch — for anyone, with or without ADHD. The "start full, then delete" premise is a design position reasoned from this research, not a finding of it. It is listed as a design position on Design decisions, defended for that reason.

    How this shaped the app

    Every trip arrives already populated, so the first action is a veto rather than an act of generation, and the trip is pre-cut into five phases so that "pack for the trip" is never the unit of work. Item groups start collapsed, and a refined trip can be saved as a template so the setup is paid for once.

    Those rows, and what backs each one, are in Design decisions, defended — Refinement over creation, Five phases per trip, Templates, and Groups collapsed by default.
