    The two shelves of apps that almost work

    Before I built Pick, Pack, Go, I went looking for the app that would let me not build it — first as an ADHD traveler trying to fix my own packing, then as someone deciding whether this thing needed to exist. I found two shelves of good software that don't meet in the middle.

    On one shelf: packing apps. Some are genuinely clever about lists. On the other: ADHD planning tools, some of them excellent — one just won Apple's iPhone App of the Year. But the packing apps weren't designed around ADHD, and the ADHD tools don't know what a trip is. So you do what people in ADHD communities describe over and over, and what I did myself: cobble both together — a packing app for the list, a visual planner for the time, sticky notes for the gaps — and abandon the whole stack mid-trip, because coordinating three tools is itself an executive function tax.

    Here's an honest look at both shelves. Everything below was re-verified against current App Store listings and official sites in August 2026 — where I couldn't confirm something, it isn't here.

    The packing apps

    PackPoint (free, $2.99 premium) had the right instinct a decade ago: answer a few questions, get an auto-generated list with weather-aware suggestions and quantities scaled to your trip length. That's real decision-load relief. But it hasn't been updated since 2023, and its structure is one flat list for one packing session — no trip timeline, no return journey, and nothing addressing memory or verification.

    Packing Pro ($2.99 one-time) is the power-user catalog: 800+ items, an "Expert Assistant" list generator, weights, values, spreadsheet export. It's also a decade of accumulated options in one interface — the exact "extreme customizability" that reads as a feature list to some people and as a wall of decisions to an overwhelmed brain. Last updated in 2022.

    Packr (free; premium from $2.99/month or $49.99 lifetime) is the best-maintained of the group — actively updated, redesigned for modern iOS, with weather-driven suggestions (10-day forecasts on premium) and multi-destination trips. If what you want is a weather-smart list generator, Packr is the strongest pick on this shelf. What it isn't: structured around executive function. One list per destination, one verification layer, quantity suggestions but no outfit planning, and its full feature set sits behind a subscription.

    PackEasy (free; Pro $1.99/month or $24.99 lifetime) is a newer entry built around a master inventory with photo attachments — a genuinely useful memory aid. It's an inventory manager at heart: no auto-generated lists, no quantity suggestions, no trip timeline. You're still constructing every list yourself, which is the blank-page problem all over again.

    What the whole shelf has in common: not one of these apps markets a single ADHD-specific feature. No second-verification pass for critical items. No concept of a trip as a timeline with lead-time tasks. And none of them models the return journey — the hotel-room repack, the highest-risk moment of the whole trip — at all.

    The ADHD tools

    Tiimo (free tier; Pro $7.99/month or $79.99/year) is a beautiful visual planner — color-coded timeline, AI task breakdown, and enough craft to win Apple's iPhone App of the Year 2025. It's also the strongest proof that ADHD-first design wins when someone commits to it. But it's a day planner. It can hold "pack" as a task; it can't tell you what to pack, how much, or whether the charger made it into the bag.

    Llama Life ($39/year after trial) nails one thing: single-tasking with a countdown timer and a small celebration when you finish. That's real ADHD insight — and it's aimed at your to-do list, with nothing trip-shaped anywhere in it. (These days it's primarily a web app.)

    Weel (free; Weel+ $7.99/month or $39.99/year) shows your whole day as a 24-hour analog dial and can attach checklists to calendar events — the closest thing on this shelf to travel support, since it also calculates travel time between events. But its checklists are flat event notes. The packing knowledge — what, how much, what's critical, what comes home — is still entirely on you.

    Things 3 ($9.99 iPhone / $19.99 iPad / $49.99 Mac, one-time) deserves its reputation: calm, focused, subscription-free. I considered building this whole concept on top of it. But it's a general to-do system — it has no native templates, no item library, no quantities, and recreating a trip's structure in it every time is exactly the setup tax an ADHD traveler can't afford.

    What this shelf has in common: these tools take ADHD seriously — and know nothing about packing. The domain knowledge (what a beach trip needs, that medication is critical, that underwear scales with days) has to come from you, every time.

    The subscription problem is an ADHD problem

    Notice the pricing column. Most ADHD-focused tools run $40–$96 a year, forever — and anyone with ADHD knows the cycle from the inside: hyperfocus-subscribe during trip planning, forget to cancel, discover the charge months later. I've paid for more forgotten subscriptions than I'll admit to in writing. A recurring bill you have to remember to evaluate is a small, permanent working-memory tax — from tools whose whole premise is reducing exactly that.

    Pick, Pack, Go is a one-time purchase. Not as a pricing gimmick, but because it's the model that respects how its users' brains work. Buy it, own it, forget about it — forgetting about it being, for once, the intended outcome. Everything runs on your device: no account, no login, nothing tracking you, and nothing I can see. Your packing library is yours.

    Where I'll point you elsewhere

    Fair's fair. If you want live weather forecasts driving your list, Packr does that and Pick, Pack, Go doesn't. If you want a visual planner for your whole day, every day, Tiimo is superb — and pairs fine with Pick, Pack, Go, since they don't overlap. And if you already run your life in Things 3 and it works, I'm the last person to talk you out of a one-time purchase.

    But if the specific thing that breaks is packing — starting it, remembering it, trusting it, timing it, and getting everything home again — that's the one job Pick, Pack, Go was built to do.
