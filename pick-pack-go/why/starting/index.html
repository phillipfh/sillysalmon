    The hardest part of packing is starting

    There's a phrase that comes up again and again when ADHDers talk about packing: "I'll stare at my empty suitcase for twenty minutes." Not packing badly — not packing at all. The suitcase is open, the trip is real, the clothes are ten feet away, and nothing happens.

    I have ADHD. I know that stare from the inside. What I didn't expect, when I started reading how other people with ADHD describe getting ready for a trip, was finding my own experience repeated almost word for word by hundreds of strangers. Packing, one of them wrote, "goes against our natural skills." The more I read, the more obvious two things became: none of this is a personal failing — and no packing app had been built with any of it in mind.

    What's actually happening

    If you're reading this for someone you love and wondering why they "just can't pack," here's the plain-language version. Packing isn't one task. It's planning (what does this trip need?), organizing (what goes together?), prioritizing (what matters most?), estimating (how much of each?), and executing (physically doing it) — all at once, with no natural starting point. That cluster of mental skills is called executive function, and it's precisely the cluster ADHD makes expensive.

    A blank packing list asks you to do the hardest part — generating structure from nothing — before you've packed a single sock. For an ADHD brain, that blank page isn't a small hurdle before the real work. It is the work, and it's where the whole thing stalls: the trip gets closer, the anxiety gets louder, and the suitcase stays empty until panic takes over the night before. I've lived that timeline more times than I want to count.

    Where most apps get this backwards

    Most packing apps are list builders. They hand you an empty list and a big library of items, and the value they offer is making it convenient to construct a list. But construction is the executive-function bottleneck. An empty list with excellent tooling is still an empty list.

    ADHD productivity apps understand initiation paralysis — many are built around it — but they're general-purpose planners. They can hold a packing list; they can't generate one. You still start from nothing, now with the added job of teaching a day-planner what a trip is.

    What Pick, Pack, Go does instead

    Pick, Pack, Go is built on a different premise: you shouldn't have to create your packing list. You should only have to refine one.

    When you create a trip, you answer a handful of questions — where, when, what kind of trip, how you're getting there — and the app builds the list for you from a library of about 840 items organized into 45 sections, with the essentials pre-selected. Your first act isn't conjuring items from a blank page. It's scanning a full list and deleting what doesn't apply. Crossing off "snorkel gear" for a work trip takes no executive function at all. That's the point.

    The structure is pre-chunked, too. A trip in Pick, Pack, Go isn't one giant list — it's five phases that mirror how a trip actually unfolds: Prep (the errands days before), Pack (the suitcase itself), Travel (the day you leave), Plans (while you're there), and Return (getting home intact). You never face "pack for the trip." You face "here's what Prep needs," which is a much smaller thing to start. Within each phase, item groups start collapsed — you expand what you're working on instead of confronting three hundred rows at once.

    And once you've refined a trip into something that fits how you actually travel, you can save it as a template. Your next similar trip starts not just from the app's defaults but from your defaults — every renamed section, every added item, every adjusted quantity, already in place. The executive-function cost of setting up a trip gets paid once, not every time.

    What Pick, Pack, Go doesn't do

    Pick, Pack, Go can't make you start. No app can — and the ones that promise to are selling something. What it can do is make the first step small enough that starting stops being the hardest part: open the app, look at a list that already exists, and delete one thing.
