    "I have plenty of time" — narrator: they did not

    The stories follow a curve so consistent you could plot it. Three days out: complete calm. The night before: "I'll pack after dinner." 11 PM: panic-packing while exhausted. And the grimmest version, told with dark humor all over ADHD communities: throwing random clothes into a backpack thirty minutes before leaving for the airport, because until that moment the flight wasn't real.

    One person estimated packing would take twenty minutes. Four hours later they were still organizing a carry-on. I recognize every point on that curve, because it's my curve too.

    What's actually happening

    Time blindness is the ADHD experience of time as binary: there's now, and there's not now. A trip that's four days away isn't "soon" — it's not now, which files it with things that don't exist yet. Then, at some point, it snaps to now, and now is far too late. Add chronically optimistic time estimates (packing feels like a twenty-minute job; it's an evening) and the night-before panic isn't a character flaw — it's the predictable output of a brain that couldn't feel the deadline approaching until it arrived.

    The cruelest part: packing rewards being spread over days. Laundry needs a day. The pharmacy closes. The mail hold needs lead time. Compressed into one panicked night, half of packing's actual tasks are simply impossible.

    Where most apps get this backwards

    Packing apps treat packing as one event — a single list, presumably completed in a single sitting, timing entirely up to you. The structure that says some of this needs to happen days before departure just isn't in the data model. ADHD time apps attack time blindness for your day in general — visual timers, schedules — but they don't know what a trip is, so it's on you to translate "pack" into scheduled, ordered steps. That translation is executive function work again, and we know how that goes.

    What Pick, Pack, Go does instead

    Pick, Pack, Go doesn't try to change your sense of time. It builds the timeline into the structure of the trip itself, so the sequencing is done before your time blindness gets a vote.

    • Five phases anchored to real days. Every trip is organized as Prep (the lead-time tasks: laundry, refills, mail hold — the things that can't be done the night before), Pack (the suitcase, the day before), Travel (day of departure), Plans (while you're there), and Return (coming home). "When should I do this?" is pre-answered by where the item lives. You never have to construct the schedule; you just work the phase you're in.
    • The app follows the trip's clock. The tab bar itself tracks where you are in the journey — once you've arrived, Prep steps aside and Return steps in; as your return date approaches, the app offers to reset Pack and Travel for the trip home. Your home screen sorts trips into current, upcoming, and past by their actual dates. The trip's timeline is visible everywhere, which is exactly what "not now" needs: reminders that now is closer than it feels.
    • Days get names, not numbers. The Plans tab's day rail is labelled with real weekdays, and an outfit you add arrives already called "Monday's Outfit" rather than "Outfit 3" — one more place where abstract trip-time gets pinned to the concrete calendar.
    • And when it is the night before — because sometimes it will be — the structure degrades gracefully. Essentials are pre-selected, critical items are badged, quantities are computed, and the Double Check still catches what matters on the way out the door. A panic-pack inside Pick, Pack, Go is still a guided pack.

    What Pick, Pack, Go doesn't do

    Pick, Pack, Go has no alarms, timers, or notifications — nothing pesters you (I know firsthand how a badly timed reminder doesn't create urgency, it just relocates the anxiety). Right now the structure is the strategy: phases that make the timeline visible instead of a schedule that nags you to feel it. Gentle, well-timed prompts may come later; if they do, they'll be built with the same care as everything else here.

    That's the problem, five ways. The other half of the story: why the apps that already exist don't solve it — the honest comparison.
