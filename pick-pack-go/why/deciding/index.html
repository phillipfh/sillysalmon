    The to-pack-or-not-to-pack debate

    One description I read called it "the dreaded 'to pack or not to pack' debate" — held separately, at full intensity, for every single item. Another person admitted packing and unpacking the same shirt five times because they couldn't commit either way. I've run that exact loop. And the numbers that come out of this are famous in ADHD spaces: fifteen tops for a three-day trip, because every individual "should I bring this?" resolved to "…better safe than sorry."

    What's actually happening

    Decisions cost something. For most people the cost of "two pairs of jeans or three?" is negligible; with ADHD, each small decision draws from the same depleted account as the big ones, and the account empties fast. When it does, there are only two cheap exits: defer the decision (bring it, just in case) or abandon the task. That's how you get both overpacked suitcases and the paralysis of a person standing motionless in front of a closet. They're the same problem wearing different outfits.

    Clothes are the worst offender because clothing decisions are combinatorial — this shirt with which pants, for which day, in which weather? Deciding item-by-item means deciding everything about the trip's wardrobe simultaneously, with no structure to lean on.

    Where most apps get this backwards

    Most packing apps are decision-neutral: they'll faithfully record whatever you decide, but the deciding is all yours. The ones that generate suggestions help at the list level, then still hand you "shirts" as a line item — quantity and combinations your problem. And a big customizable item catalog, the flagship feature of the power-user packing apps, is more choices, not fewer. For an ADHD user, an app proud of offering options for everything is describing the disease and calling it the cure.

    What Pick, Pack, Go does instead

    Its core stance — start from a full list and delete — is itself a decision-shrinking move: veto decisions ("not this") are far cheaper than generative ones ("what do I need?"). But three features go after decision load directly:

    • Relevance filtering means most decisions never reach you. Every section and item in the app's library is tagged for when it applies — trip type, weather, season, transportation, trip length. Tell Pick, Pack, Go it's a summer beach weekend by car, and the international-travel documents, ski gear, and extended-stay errands simply don't come up. You can't agonize over an option you were never shown.
    • Quantities come pre-decided. Items carry formulas — days-plus-a-buffer, one-per-trip — so a five-day trip proposes five-day numbers. "How many?" arrives answered; you adjust only if you disagree, which is a much smaller decision than producing a number from nothing.
    • Outfit planning turns "what am I bringing?" into "what am I wearing Tuesday?" On the Plans tab you name an outfit and put clothes in it — a question with a shape, a scope and an end, instead of the combinatorial one. It's the digital version of the outfit-stacking strategy ADHDers already invented for themselves. Two things about how it lands are deliberate. First, the garments file onto your packing list by type, not by outfit, because when you're actually packing you're standing at a drawer, not at a day — an earlier version of this arranged the list by outfit and sent you back to the same drawer six times. Second, naming a specific shirt lowers the generic shirt count by one rather than replacing it, so your total stays honest whether you plan every day or exactly one. And a day you never planned costs you nothing: the generic counts are still sitting there, unchanged.

    Everything stays overridable, and nothing is all-or-nothing. Plan one outfit for the rehearsal dinner and ignore the rest of the trip; double the sock formula; add the kit the filters didn't suggest — the defaults are opinions, not rules. The goal was never to take decisions away from you; it's to stop the app from handing you decisions it could have made itself.

    What Pick, Pack, Go doesn't do

    Pick, Pack, Go won't tell you which shirt. It narrows every question until what's left is the part only you can answer — and small enough to actually answer it.

    There's one more thief to deal with, and it steals the thing packing needs most: time.
